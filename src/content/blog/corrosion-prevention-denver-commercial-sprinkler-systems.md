Corrosion is the leading cause of unplanned fire sprinkler system failures in commercial buildings in the United States. Not fires, not physical damage, not equipment failure at the valve level. Corrosion. It develops slowly, over years, in ways that aren't visible from the outside of the pipe, and it shows up as a pinhole leak in a ceiling, a failed system component, or a deficiency finding during the internal pipe inspection that NFPA 25 requires at the five-year and ten-year marks.

For Denver commercial building owners, understanding how corrosion develops and what can be done to slow or prevent it is a practical matter, not a theoretical one. The systems protecting your building are almost certainly experiencing corrosion right now. The question is whether it's being managed or ignored.

## How Corrosion Develops in Sprinkler Piping

Standard commercial sprinkler systems use black steel pipe. Black steel corrodes when it's exposed to oxygen and moisture, which is precisely what a typical wet pipe system provides: water in contact with steel, with dissolved oxygen in that water and oxygen in the air space at the top of each pipe segment.

The corrosion process consumes the oxygen from the water and the trapped air pockets in the piping, producing iron oxide, which is rust. That rust accumulates as deposits on the interior pipe surfaces, as sediment that collects at low points, and as nodules that can eventually penetrate through the pipe wall.

**Microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC)** is a more aggressive form that shows up in Denver commercial buildings regularly. MIC occurs when certain bacteria colonize the interior of a sprinkler pipe. These bacteria accelerate the corrosion process dramatically, producing pitting and through-wall failures at a much faster rate than normal electrochemical corrosion. MIC is more common in systems that have been modified or refilled multiple times, that have been impaired and recharged, or that share water supply with systems that have experienced MIC previously.

A system with active MIC can develop through-wall pitting in black steel pipe within two to four years of initial colonization. The failures appear as small, targeted leaks with no other visible signs of system distress.

## What Internal Inspections Find

[NFPA 25](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-25-standard-for-the-inspection-testing-and-maintenance-of-water-based-fire-protection-systems/about) requires internal inspections of sprinkler system piping at five and ten years after initial installation, and every five years thereafter. These inspections involve opening or cutting into the system piping to visually assess the interior pipe condition and to look for obstructions, sediment, and biological growth.

What those inspections find in Denver commercial buildings spans a wide range. Many systems show moderate surface rust and some sediment, which is expected and manageable. Systems that have experienced MIC often show aggressive pitting, heavy biological deposits, and in some cases through-wall perforations at the inspection point. Systems that haven't been flushed, that have absorbed organic material through open connections, or that are connected to a supply with high biological load can show heavy obstruction-level deposits.

The five-year internal inspection is not just a paperwork requirement. It's the mechanism that catches developing corrosion problems before they become failures during a fire event or an unexpected midnight leak.

## Nitrogen Supervisory Systems

The most effective tool for slowing oxygen-driven corrosion in steel sprinkler piping is replacing the trapped air in the system with nitrogen. This is called a nitrogen supervisory system, or nitrogen blanketing.

Standard wet pipe systems have small air pockets at high points in the piping. That air is roughly 21 percent oxygen, and that oxygen is available to react with the steel pipe and the water. Corrosion in a wet pipe system is partly driven by that trapped oxygen being consumed over time.

Nitrogen supervisory systems continuously purge the air from the high points in the sprinkler system and replace it with nitrogen, which is inert. By maintaining a nitrogen atmosphere in the air spaces rather than an oxygen-containing one, the corrosion-driving mechanism is significantly reduced. The water in the system still contains some dissolved oxygen, but the removal of the trapped air pockets substantially reduces the available oxygen for the corrosion reaction.

Nitrogen supervisory systems are not a complete prevention, but the evidence from long-term monitoring of commercial systems is that they meaningfully slow the progression of both standard electrochemical corrosion and MIC. Buildings that have installed nitrogen supervision after experiencing corrosion-related failures typically see slower progression at subsequent internal inspections.

For dry pipe systems, the same principle applies with an additional benefit: nitrogen supervision eliminates moisture from condensation that would otherwise accumulate in the dry piping, reducing the water-at-low-points issue that causes freeze damage.

## What Denver Buildings Should Do

If your building's sprinkler system has never had an internal obstruction inspection, and your system is more than five years old, that inspection is overdue under NFPA 25 regardless of whether anyone has flagged it. Scheduling that inspection gives you a baseline for the current interior condition.

If your system has had prior corrosion-related issues, pinhole leaks, pipe replacements driven by pitting, or biological deposits found at previous inspections, nitrogen supervision is worth evaluating. It's an ongoing cost, but it's modest relative to the cost of emergency pipe repairs, water damage cleanup, and the system downtime that comes with an unexpected failure.

If you've never received an obstruction investigation finding from an inspection contractor and your system is older than ten years, that may mean the inspection was never performed rather than meaning the system is clean. Confirm with your records rather than assuming.

## Why This Matters for Your Coverage

Insurance carriers, particularly those with engineering-based inspection programs, increasingly include internal pipe condition and obstruction investigation status in their evaluation of a property's fire protection maintenance. A system that has never been internally inspected and is more than ten years old can generate a recommendation from a carrier that affects your coverage terms.

Keeping up with the NFPA 25 internal inspection schedule is both a compliance matter and a property management one.
