If you manage a hospital, nursing home, or other Medicare-certified facility in Colorado, the fire and life safety code you get surveyed against has been frozen since 2016. That may finally change. In November, CMS proposed adopting the 2024 editions of NFPA 101 (the Life Safety Code) and NFPA 99 (the Health Care Facilities Code), replacing the 2012 editions it has enforced for a decade. Here's what the proposal says, what it doesn't say yet, and how Front Range healthcare facility managers should prepare.

## What CMS Actually Proposed

The proposal is part of a larger rule CMS calls "Cutting Administrative Requirements for Excellence in Patient Care." It was [published in the Federal Register on November 10](https://www.hfmmagazine.com/cms-proposes-updates-conditions-participation-code-set), and the comment period runs until **January 11, 2027**. According to [Fire & Safety Journal Americas](https://fireandsafetyjournalamericas.com/cms-nfpa-code-healthcare/), the change would touch more than 20,000 hospitals, nursing homes, and other healthcare facilities nationwide.

The headline items:

- **NFPA 101 and NFPA 99** would move from the 2012 editions to the 2024 editions.
- **NFPA 110**, the standard for emergency and standby power systems, would be updated to the 2022 edition, per [HFM Magazine](https://www.hfmmagazine.com/cms-proposes-updates-conditions-participation-code-set).
- CMS would formally write into its rules the authority to let facilities follow specific provisions of newer NFPA editions, something it has handled case by case in the past.

**No compliance date has been set.** CMS has said it will establish one in the final rule, and it hasn't announced when that final rule will come out. Nothing changes for your next survey because of this proposal alone.

## Why It Matters That CMS Is a Decade Behind

The Life Safety Code is updated every three years. Since 2016, NFPA has published the 2015, 2018, 2021, and 2024 editions, but CMS surveyors and accrediting organizations have kept enforcing 2012. Colorado facilities have been living with that gap the whole time: designers and local fire departments often work from newer codes, while the federal survey checks against older ones.

That mismatch shows up in real ways. A remodel gets designed to current code, then the facility discovers a federal requirement from 2012 still applies. Or the facility pays for testing work that the newer editions no longer require. Moving CMS closer to current editions narrows that gap.

## What Changes in the 2024 Edition

[Code Red Consultants](https://coderedconsultants.com/insights/cms-proposes-adoption-2024-editions-nfpa-101-99/) summarized several changes healthcare facilities would notice:

- **Larger smoke compartments.** Healthcare smoke compartments could be up to 40,000 square feet, up from 22,500.
- **Smoke barriers below healthcare floors.** The 2024 edition removes the requirement for smoke barriers on a story below healthcare occupancies.
- **Bigger suites.** Certain healthcare suites could be up to 15,000 square feet.
- **Exit sign testing.** The monthly exit sign test requirement goes away for most LED signs, a change that started in the 2021 edition.
- **Fire alarm notification.** Private-mode notification is addressed more clearly.

Several of those changes depend directly on your sprinkler system. The Life Safety Code allows larger compartments and fewer barriers in healthcare occupancies largely because those buildings are fully sprinklered and the system is expected to work. **More design flexibility puts more weight on a sprinkler system that's actually maintained.**

## Where Your Sprinkler System Fits In

The Life Safety Code doesn't spell out sprinkler inspection, testing, and maintenance itself. It points to NFPA 25, and each edition of NFPA 101 references a specific edition of NFPA 25. Under the 2012 Life Safety Code, that's the 2011 edition of NFPA 25, which is why surveyors have been checking healthcare ITM against a standard that's 15 years old.

The sources covering the proposal haven't detailed how the NFPA 25 reference would change, so we're not going to guess at specific new requirements. But if CMS moves to the 2024 Life Safety Code, it's reasonable to expect your ITM program will eventually be checked against a newer NFPA 25. We've covered what's changed in recent editions in our posts on the [2026 NFPA 25 updates](/blog/nfpa-25-2026-updates-denver-property-managers/) and the [new deficiency deadlines](/blog/new-nfpa-25-deficiency-deadlines-denver/).

For healthcare facilities, the sprinkler-related items surveyors already focus on include:

- Quarterly and annual inspection and testing records, complete and easy to find
- Fire pump weekly or monthly churn tests and the annual flow test
- Five-year internal pipe assessments and obstruction investigations
- Impairment procedures, including fire watch documentation
- Spare sprinkler cabinets stocked with the right heads and wrenches

None of that goes away under a newer edition. Records that are already clean and current will carry over easily to whatever standard ends up applying.

## What Front Range Healthcare Facility Managers Should Do Now

You don't need to change anything this week, but this is a good time to get ahead of it:

1. **Read the proposal and consider commenting.** Comments reference file code CMS-3484-P and are due January 11, 2027. If a specific 2012 requirement has caused your facility trouble, this is the place to say so.
2. **Audit your ITM records.** Pull the last 12 months of sprinkler, fire pump, and standpipe reports. Look for missing quarterlies, open deficiencies, and anything a surveyor could flag. If you have a backlog, our [service and repair team](/services/service-and-repair/) can work through it.
3. **Check your fire pump program.** Hospitals in taller Denver and Aurora buildings often rely on fire pumps. Make sure your weekly or monthly runs are logged and your annual test is current. Our [fire pump testing page](/services/fire-pump-testing/) covers what that test involves.
4. **Hold off on renovation shortcuts.** Don't design a project around the 2024 smoke compartment sizes until a final rule and compliance date are published. Until then, 2012 is what gets surveyed.
5. **Talk to your accrediting organization.** Ask how they plan to handle the transition once CMS finalizes it.

## What This Means for Your Building

If you run a healthcare facility on the Front Range, this proposal is good news in the long run. It brings federal surveys closer to the codes your architects and local fire marshals already use, and it may cut some testing that no longer makes sense. But the timing is open, and your sprinkler system still has to pass under today's rules. Use the comment period to tighten up your inspection records, close out open deficiencies, and make sure your fire pump and impairment documentation would hold up to a surveyor tomorrow.
