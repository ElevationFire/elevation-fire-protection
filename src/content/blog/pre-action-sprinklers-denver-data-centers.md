Most commercial buildings in Denver have one of two sprinkler system types: wet pipe, where the piping stays filled with water at all times, or dry pipe, where the piping holds pressurized air until a head activates. Those two types cover the vast majority of occupied space on the Front Range. But a third type, the pre-action system, shows up in a specific category of building where the consequences of an accidental water discharge are severe enough to justify a more complex design.

If you manage or own a building with a data center, server room, archival storage, museum collection, or high-value electronics, it's worth understanding what a pre-action system does, how it's maintained, and where the requirements differ from what you're used to.

## Why Some Spaces Can't Use Wet or Dry Pipe

In most commercial spaces, an accidental sprinkler discharge is costly but survivable. Equipment gets wet, the system gets reset, and normal operations resume after cleanup. In some spaces, the contents being protected are worth far more than the building itself, and water damage is catastrophic regardless of fire involvement.

A data center with active servers, storage, and network infrastructure can sustain more damage from a single inadvertent discharge than from many fires. Museum collections and archival materials can be irreparably harmed by water contact. High-value electronics fabrication areas share the same vulnerability.

In these spaces, the tradeoff changes. The question is no longer "how fast can we get water on the fire" but "how do we ensure water only reaches this space when there is definitely a fire, and not from a mechanical failure, a physically damaged sprinkler head, or a faulty alarm."

That's the problem pre-action systems are designed to solve.

## How a Pre-Action System Works

A pre-action system looks like a dry pipe system in one important way: the piping is filled with air or nitrogen rather than water under normal conditions. No water is in the distribution piping until the system is deliberately activated.

The difference is in what triggers water to enter the system. In a dry pipe system, a sprinkler head activating releases air pressure, which trips the dry pipe valve and allows water in. The activation of the sprinkler head alone is what admits water.

In a pre-action system, there is a separate pre-action valve that controls water entry into the piping. That valve requires a signal from a detection system before it opens. The detection system is typically a network of heat detectors, smoke detectors, or both, covering the protected space.

**Single interlock pre-action** requires the detection system to alarm before the pre-action valve opens. A sprinkler head can activate independently without admitting water if the detection system hasn't alarmed first. This configuration protects against accidental head activation: a physically damaged or thermally stressed head discharging into a server room won't flood the room unless the detection system also sees a fire condition.

**Double interlock pre-action** requires both the detection system to alarm AND a sprinkler head to activate before the pre-action valve opens. This provides maximum protection against accidental discharge, since both an independent detection event and a head activation must occur simultaneously before water enters the piping. The tradeoff is response time: water cannot reach an open head until both conditions are satisfied.

For most Denver data centers and server rooms, single interlock pre-action is the more common choice. Double interlock is used in spaces where even a brief inadvertent flow is unacceptable.

## NFPA Requirements for Pre-Action Systems

[NFPA 13](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-13-standard-for-the-installation-of-sprinkler-systems/about) governs pre-action system installation and design. The detection system design requirements, the pre-action valve specifications, and the interaction between the fire alarm and sprinkler control systems are all governed by this standard, in coordination with [NFPA 72](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-72-national-fire-alarm-and-signaling-code/about) for the detection side.

Under [NFPA 25](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-25-standard-for-the-inspection-testing-and-maintenance-of-water-based-fire-protection-systems/about), pre-action systems have inspection and testing requirements that go beyond what either wet or dry systems require. The detection system components must be tested, the pre-action valve must be trip-tested annually, and the interaction between the detection system and the sprinkler control system must be verified to function correctly as a unit. This is not a standard wet pipe inspection.

One issue that shows up regularly in Denver buildings with pre-action systems is a disconnect between who is responsible for testing what. The fire sprinkler contractor handles the sprinkler-side components and the pre-action valve. The fire alarm contractor handles the detection system. The integration testing, which verifies that the two systems actually communicate correctly and that the pre-action valve responds to the detection system as designed, requires both contractors to be present and to run a coordinated test. Buildings that schedule each system's inspection separately, without a coordinated integration test, often have incomplete documentation and may have undiscovered failures in the system interface.

## Common Pre-Action System Locations in Denver Buildings

Pre-action systems on the Front Range appear most often in:

- **Data centers and server rooms** in office buildings, hospitals, and standalone facilities, including smaller IT rooms that have been built out within larger commercial buildings
- **Cold storage and refrigerated warehouse spaces** where the combination of freezing temperatures and high-value contents makes wet pipe impractical and dry pipe insufficient
- **Art storage and museum collections** in Denver's growing arts district and institutional buildings
- **Telecommunications equipment rooms** in larger commercial and mixed-use buildings
- **Archival storage** in government buildings, law firms, and medical facilities where records must be preserved

If your building has undergone a data center or server room build-out at any point, it may have a pre-action system that isn't reflected in your general building inspection documentation.
