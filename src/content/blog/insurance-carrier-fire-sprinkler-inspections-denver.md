Most Denver commercial property owners think about fire sprinkler compliance in terms of the AHJ: the Authority Having Jurisdiction, which in Denver is the Denver Fire Department. Keep the system maintained, pass the NFPA 25 inspection, stay in good standing with the AHJ, and you're compliant. That's accurate as far as it goes.

What it misses is that your insurance carrier may have its own inspection program, its own set of standards, and its own compliance requirements, and those requirements can differ from what NFPA 25 and the Denver Fire Department require. For commercial properties with certain insurers, carrier inspections are not optional and not interchangeable with NFPA 25 inspections.

Understanding both programs is the difference between being compliant with the code and being compliant with everything that governs your property.

## Why Carriers Run Their Own Inspections

Property insurance carriers have a financial stake in your building's fire protection that's separate from the AHJ's regulatory role. When a fire causes a major loss, the carrier pays the claim. That economic exposure creates a direct incentive for carriers to understand the actual condition of the fire protection systems they're insuring.

Large commercial insurers, particularly those with factory mutual or industrial property programs, developed their own engineering and inspection standards independently of the NFPA code cycle. Some of those standards are more stringent than NFPA in specific areas. Others focus on risks that NFPA 25 addresses generally but that carriers have identified as higher-frequency loss drivers based on their own claims data.

The result is that some Denver commercial properties are subject to two parallel inspection regimes: an NFPA 25-based program that satisfies the AHJ, and a carrier-based program that satisfies the insurer's requirements for continued coverage.

## FM Global and Their Standards

FM Global is the most prominent carrier with an independent inspection program in the commercial and industrial market. If your property is insured through an FM Global policy, you likely have or will have FM-employed engineers and inspectors visiting your site.

FM Global publishes its own technical standards, known as Loss Prevention Data Sheets, which cover fire protection system design, installation, and maintenance. These data sheets are not identical to NFPA standards. In some areas, FM Global's requirements exceed NFPA minimums: more frequent testing, higher design densities for specific occupancy types, or additional installation requirements that go beyond the NFPA base code.

FM Global inspections result in Improvement Recommendations: items the carrier has identified as conditions that increase loss potential. These recommendations carry compliance deadlines. Missing those deadlines can affect your coverage terms at renewal, trigger premium adjustments, or in some cases result in coverage restrictions.

Building owners who receive an FM inspection report and file it without acting on the recommendations sometimes discover at renewal that those open items have consequences. The recommendations are not suggestions.

## Zurich, Travelers, and Mid-Market Carriers

FM Global's program is the most extensive, but other large commercial carriers have inspection programs as well. Zurich, Travelers, and other insurers writing commercial property coverage in Denver may conduct periodic site inspections through their own engineering departments or through third-party inspection services they engage.

The scope and frequency of these inspections varies by carrier, by policy type, and by the specifics of your property. A large industrial warehouse will typically see more carrier attention than a small professional office building. High-value contents, special hazards, or significant loss history will increase the frequency and depth of carrier review.

If you're not sure whether your carrier conducts inspections, ask your broker or review your policy documents. Carriers that perform inspections typically include language in the policy or the policy terms that describes the inspection program and your obligations when a deficiency or recommendation is identified.

## Where Carrier and NFPA Requirements Diverge

The most common areas where carrier requirements and NFPA 25 requirements produce different obligations for Denver property owners:

**Testing frequency.** Some carriers require certain components to be tested more frequently than the NFPA 25 baseline. A carrier might require quarterly fire pump testing where NFPA 25 requires annual, or might require more frequent obstruction investigations on systems with a history of corrosion.

**Documentation requirements.** Carriers often require specific documentation formats, sign-off by licensed contractors, and submission of test reports to the carrier directly in addition to maintaining them on site. NFPA 25 specifies what records must be kept; carrier requirements may specify how they must be reported.

**Sprinkler system design and coverage.** When a building's occupancy changes, carriers sometimes require a formal review of whether the existing sprinkler system design is adequate for the new use, even if the AHJ hasn't flagged the change. A warehouse that converted to storage of commodities requiring a higher sprinkler density may satisfy the AHJ's inspection requirements while still having an open carrier Improvement Recommendation about the system's design adequacy for the current storage configuration.

**Response timelines.** When the AHJ and a carrier have both identified the same deficiency, the carrier's correction timeline may be shorter. When they conflict, the stricter deadline controls. If you're managing a deficiency that both the AHJ and your carrier have cited, verify which timeline applies to each party and work to the more demanding one.

## What to Do If You Receive a Carrier Inspection Report

Treat a carrier inspection report the same way you treat an NFPA 25 deficiency report from your sprinkler contractor: document it, assign ownership for each item, and track completion against the deadlines given.

For items that require a licensed fire protection contractor to correct, get that work scheduled early enough to allow for any lead time on parts or scheduling. Don't wait until the deadline week to discover the work takes longer than expected.

Notify your broker when significant Improvement Recommendations are open. Your broker is your advocate with the carrier, and they should be aware of open items so they can help you manage the communication with the insurer at renewal.

If you're not sure how to interpret a specific carrier recommendation in terms of what work is actually required, your fire protection contractor can help translate the technical language into a scope of work and timeline.
