A lot of Denver commercial and mixed-use building owners know they have a fire sprinkler system and roughly understand what it does. Fewer know that their building also has a standpipe system, that the two serve completely different functions, and that the inspection and maintenance requirements are distinct. If your building is four stories or taller, has a parking structure, or was built before modern sprinkler codes were adopted, there's a reasonable chance you have both.

## What a Standpipe System Does

A sprinkler system is designed to control or suppress a fire automatically without requiring anyone to operate it. Sprinkler heads activate individually in response to heat and discharge water directly onto the fire below them.

A standpipe system is designed to give the fire department and, in some configurations, building occupants a water supply connection point closer to where a fire is burning. Rather than fire department apparatus pumping water from the street through hundreds of feet of hose up stairwells, the standpipe delivers water through a fixed system of piping inside the building, with hose connections at each floor level or at key locations throughout the structure.

In a high-rise fire in Denver, the standpipe is what makes the upper floors accessible to firefighters in a reasonable amount of time. In a large parking garage, it provides the water supply coverage that the layout of the structure makes difficult to deliver from the exterior.

## Three Classes of Standpipe

[NFPA 14](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-14-standard-for-the-installation-of-standpipe-and-hose-systems/about), which governs standpipe design and installation, defines three classes based on who is intended to use the system.

**Class I standpipes** are designed for fire department use only. They have 2.5-inch hose connections that accept fire department hose and provide the high flow rates and pressures that engine crews need. Class I systems are the most common in Denver mid-rise and high-rise commercial buildings. Building occupants don't use them.

**Class II standpipes** are designed for building occupant use, typically with a 1.5-inch hose station and a preconnected hose in a cabinet. The intent is to let trained building staff attack a small fire before the fire department arrives. Class II systems are less common in modern Denver commercial buildings because the assumption that occupants will effectively fight fires before evacuating has been challenged. Many newer codes focus on evacuation rather than occupant firefighting.

**Class III standpipes** combine both: a 2.5-inch fire department connection and a 1.5-inch occupant hose station at the same location. Class III systems are found in buildings where both capabilities were required at the time of construction.

Knowing which class your building has matters because the inspection requirements differ, the components differ, and the maintenance scope is different.

## How Standpipes Differ From Sprinkler Systems

This is where building owners sometimes get into trouble: they assume their sprinkler inspection covers the standpipe, or that the same contractor is responsible for both under a single contract. That may or may not be true depending on how the inspection agreement is written.

Under [NFPA 25](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-25-standard-for-the-inspection-testing-and-maintenance-of-water-based-fire-protection-systems/about), standpipes have their own inspection, testing, and maintenance schedule. Quarterly visual inspections, annual inspections, and five-year flow tests are required separately from whatever your sprinkler system schedule looks like. The five-year flow test is the item most often missing from buildings that otherwise keep up with annual inspections.

A five-year flow test requires flowing water through the standpipe at the design demand, measuring pressure and flow at the most remote and hydraulically challenging hose connection in the building, and verifying that the system can deliver what it's designed to deliver. This test requires temporarily taking floors out of service, coordinating water discharge locations, and usually requires a licensed contractor with a flow test pump. It is not a quick inspection item.

For Denver mid-rise buildings, the five-year flow test cycle is a common gap. Buildings that have been inspected annually for years sometimes have no record of a five-year flow test ever being completed, either because the building changed hands without records being transferred or because the testing was simply never scheduled.

## Common Standpipe Deficiencies in Denver Buildings

After servicing standpipe systems across the Front Range, the same issues come up repeatedly.

**Hose valves that won't fully open or close.** Hose valves at each floor landing see very little use and often haven't been operated in years. When they seize or become difficult to operate, it creates a problem in the middle of an emergency. NFPA 25 requires hose valves to be exercised annually to verify operability.

**Missing hose valve caps.** Like FDC caps, hose valve outlet caps keep debris out of the system piping at floor-level connections. Missing caps are a common finding, particularly in mechanical rooms and stairwells where custodial staff may have removed them and not replaced them.

**Pressure reducing valves out of range.** Taller buildings need pressure-reducing valves at lower floor hose connections to keep outlet pressures within a range that firefighters can safely handle. These valves require separate testing to verify they're set correctly. A PRV that has drifted or failed can deliver too much pressure, too little pressure, or no flow at all.

**No documentation of five-year testing.** This comes up frequently on Denver buildings that have been through multiple property management changes. Without records, it's impossible to know the last time the system was flow-tested to its design demand.

## What This Means for Your Building

If you're not sure whether your building has a standpipe, that's worth finding out. The building's original construction drawings, your certificate of occupancy, and any existing inspection reports should identify what systems are present. If your building is taller than three or four stories, has a large parking structure, or was built in an era when standpipes were required in that occupancy type, it likely has one.

If you have a standpipe and can't locate a record of a five-year flow test, that's a gap worth addressing before the Denver Fire Department identifies it during their own inspection process.
