Denver's warehouse and distribution market has grown significantly over the past several years, and the fire protection implications of that growth aren't always visible until there's a problem. The fire sprinkler system installed in a building when it was first permitted was designed for a specific occupancy, a specific storage configuration, and a specific fire load. When any of those change, the system may no longer meet the design requirements for the space it's protecting, and in many cases the building owner has no idea.

This isn't a niche regulatory concern. It's one of the most common compliance gaps in Denver's commercial and industrial building stock, and it comes up every time a warehouse changes tenants, adds rack storage, increases storage heights, or shifts from one commodity type to another.

## Why Storage Configuration Affects Sprinkler Design

Sprinkler systems are hydraulically designed to deliver a specific density of water over a specific area in response to a fire. That design is based on assumptions about how fast a fire will grow, how much heat it will generate, and how high the fire will be before the sprinklers activate.

Those assumptions are directly tied to what's being stored in the building and how it's stored.

[NFPA 13](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-13-standard-for-the-installation-of-sprinkler-systems/about), which governs sprinkler system design and installation, classifies storage occupancies by commodity type and configuration. A building storing Class I commodities — non-combustible products in wood or cardboard containers, stored on pallets in solid piles — has a substantially lower fire load than the same building storing Class IV commodities: products with significant plastics content, stored in rack storage systems that channel fire vertically and allow it to spread through the racking structure.

The difference in required sprinkler density between those two scenarios is not minor. A system designed for palletized Class I storage at 20 feet may be completely inadequate for rack storage of plastics-containing products at the same height, and a system designed for one may not protect effectively against the other.

## What Triggers a Sprinkler Design Review

The practical question for Denver warehouse owners and operators is what actually requires them to revisit the sprinkler design. The triggers under NFPA 13 and local AHJ requirements include:

**Storage height increases.** If your tenant has started storing product higher than the building's original design height, the existing sprinkler system may not be adequate. NFPA 13 sets maximum storage heights for each commodity class and system design. Exceeding those heights without a design review is a code violation.

**Introduction of rack storage.** A building that was originally designed for palletized floor storage and has since had rack storage installed has a materially different fire hazard. Rack storage creates vertical channels that allow fire to travel rapidly upward through the storage array, heating and activating sprinklers faster and over a larger area. In-rack sprinklers, located within the rack structure itself, may be required depending on the storage height and commodity class.

**Commodity class changes.** A warehouse that shifts from storing non-combustible products to storing products with significant plastic packaging, foam components, or other high-heat-release materials has moved to a higher commodity classification. The original sprinkler design may not be rated for the new commodity class.

**Addition of mezzanines or storage platforms.** Building out a mezzanine or elevated storage platform adds a level of storage that the original design didn't account for and may require additional sprinkler coverage above and below the platform.

**Tenant changes.** A change of tenant doesn't require a building permit, but it may bring a change in what's being stored and how. Property owners who lease warehouse space to multiple tenants over time can end up with a building where the current occupancy has drifted far from what the sprinkler system was designed to protect.

## What Denver's AHJ Looks For

The Denver Fire Department reviews storage configurations as part of their occupancy inspection program. When an inspector identifies storage that exceeds the design parameters of the existing sprinkler system, they can issue a Notice and Order requiring the building owner to either modify the storage to stay within design limits or commission a new sprinkler design evaluation.

For property owners who aren't sure where the design limits are, the easiest first step is locating the sprinkler system's hydraulic calculation documents. These documents, required under NFPA 13 at the time of installation and typically kept on site in the fire riser room, specify the design density, the design area, the commodity classification the system was designed for, and the maximum storage height the design supports. If those documents can't be found, a licensed fire protection engineer or contractor can often reconstruct the design parameters from the system as-installed.

## In-Rack Sprinklers: When They're Required

For storage that exceeds certain heights under NFPA 13, ceiling sprinklers alone are not sufficient. The standard requires in-rack sprinklers: heads positioned within the rack structure itself, typically at defined vertical intervals, to intercept a fire before it has climbed through the full height of the racking.

In-rack sprinkler installation is a more involved project than adding ceiling heads. It requires a hydraulic design that accounts for the interaction between the in-rack system and the ceiling system, coordination with the racking configuration, and connections back to the building's existing water supply. It also typically requires an AHJ review and permit.

The need for in-rack sprinklers is determined by commodity class, storage height, and rack configuration. Not every racked warehouse requires them, but the analysis needs to be done correctly rather than assumed.

## A Practical Step for Denver Warehouse Owners

If your building is used for warehousing or distribution and you don't have a copy of the hydraulic calculations from the original sprinkler installation, getting those documents should be the first step. If the documents show the system was designed for a commodity class or storage height that your current tenant has exceeded, you have actionable information about where you stand.

If you're in the process of leasing to a new tenant, adding storage equipment, or making changes to the building layout, a fire protection review before the work begins is significantly less expensive than a redesign and retrofit after the AHJ has issued a notice.
