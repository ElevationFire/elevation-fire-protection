Most commercial building owners in Denver know they have a fire sprinkler system. Fewer know which type they have, and almost none know that the difference between a wet pipe and dry pipe system affects how fast the system responds, how it needs to be maintained, and which parts of their building each type can legally protect. If your building has unheated spaces, a parking structure, a loading dock, or an exterior canopy, you may have both, and the distinction matters more than most people realize.

## How Wet Pipe Systems Work

A wet pipe sprinkler system is exactly what the name suggests: the distribution piping is filled with pressurized water at all times. When a sprinkler head activates (each head operates independently based on the heat it senses directly above it), water discharges immediately. There's no delay.

Wet pipe systems are the most common type in commercial buildings for good reason. They're mechanically simple, they require less maintenance than the alternatives, and their response time is as fast as a sprinkler system can be. For any space in your building that's maintained above 40 degrees Fahrenheit year-round, a wet pipe system is almost always the right choice.

The limitation is the temperature requirement. Water in the pipes freezes. A wet pipe system installed in a space that drops below 40°F is a system waiting to fail.

## How Dry Pipe Systems Work

A dry pipe system holds the distribution piping under pressurized air or nitrogen instead of water. A specialized valve (the dry pipe valve) sits between the water supply and the dry piping. When a sprinkler head activates, it releases the air pressure in the pipe above it. That pressure drop trips the dry pipe valve, water enters the system, and discharges from the open head.

The tradeoff is time. From the moment a sprinkler activates to when water reaches the head, there's a delay, typically 30 to 60 seconds, while air exhausts and water travels through the piping. [NFPA 13](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-13-standard-for-the-installation-of-sprinkler-systems/about), which governs sprinkler system design and installation, sets specific requirements on water delivery time to limit how long that delay can run.

For the spaces where dry pipe systems are appropriate, that delay is an acceptable tradeoff for the ability to protect areas that would destroy a wet pipe system.

## Where Each System Belongs on the Front Range

Denver's climate makes this question more consequential than it is in warmer cities. The Front Range reliably drops below 40°F from October through March, and unheated building spaces can hit those temperatures on cold nights even earlier in the fall and later in the spring.

**Wet pipe** is the correct choice for conditioned interior spaces: offices, retail floors, hotel rooms, hospital wards, multi-family residential units, and any space with consistent heating. The overwhelming majority of the square footage in a typical Denver commercial building should be on a wet pipe system.

**Dry pipe** is used where wet pipe physically cannot be. The most common applications in Front Range buildings include:

- **Parking garages**, both attached and standalone, where maintaining 40°F across the full structure is impractical
- **Loading docks and receiving areas** with large exterior doors that are open for extended periods
- **Unheated warehouses and cold storage** facilities
- **Exterior covered walkways and canopies** attached to the building
- **Attic spaces** above insulated ceilings in buildings where the attic isn't conditioned

Many Denver commercial buildings have mixed systems: wet pipe throughout the conditioned interior, and one or more dry pipe zones covering the garage level, the loading area, or the roof mechanical penthouse. Managing both types in the same building requires understanding the separate inspection requirements each carries.

## Maintenance and Inspection Differences

Dry pipe systems require meaningfully more maintenance than wet pipe systems, and the inspections are more involved.

Under [NFPA 25](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-25-standard-for-the-inspection-testing-and-maintenance-of-water-based-fire-protection-systems/about), dry pipe systems have several requirements that wet pipe systems don't:

**Annual trip test.** The dry pipe valve must be tripped annually to verify it operates correctly, water fills the system, and the system drains properly. This test takes the zone offline and requires a licensed contractor to perform and reset.

**Air compressor or nitrogen supply maintenance.** Dry systems require a pressurized supply to maintain the air charge in the piping. The compressor or nitrogen supply must be inspected and maintained to keep the system in supervisory condition.

**Low-point drain checks.** Dry pipe systems accumulate small amounts of water over time from condensation and minor valve weeping. That water collects at low points in the piping and must be drained, particularly before temperatures drop below freezing. A dry pipe system with undrained water at a low point can freeze and rupture in the same cold snap it was designed to protect against.

**Accelerators.** Many dry pipe systems include an accelerator, a device that speeds up the trip time by rapidly venting air from the system when a head opens. Accelerators require separate inspection and testing to verify they function correctly without tripping the system prematurely.

Property managers who assume their dry pipe zone is maintained under the same checklist as the wet pipe interior are often surprised when a full NFPA 25 inspection surfaces a backlog of dry-system-specific items.

## Common Mistakes on Mixed Systems

Buildings with both wet and wet pipe systems create a few recurring problems.

**Assuming the whole building is one system type.** Modification work on a wet pipe zone, including adding or relocating heads, cannot automatically be extended into a dry pipe zone without accounting for air volume, trip time calculations, and valve capacity. Work that's straightforward in the wet zone requires more analysis in the dry zone.

**Missing the pre-winter low-point drain.** Every fall, the dry pipe zones in your building should be checked for accumulated water at low points before sustained freezing temperatures arrive. This is a straightforward task, but it's the kind of item that falls through the cracks on buildings where the sprinkler system doesn't have a dedicated maintenance schedule.

**Incorrect identification during emergencies.** When a fire department or building engineer is troubleshooting an alarm or a system activation, knowing which zones are wet and which are dry matters immediately. Clear system maps and zone labeling prevent confusion.

## What This Means for Your Building

If you're not sure whether your building has wet pipe, dry pipe, or a combination of both, your sprinkler inspection report should show it. If you don't have a recent inspection report, or if you've made changes to your building's heated zones since the system was last reviewed, that's worth addressing before the next heating season.
