Aurora's industrial and warehouse corridor has kept general contractors busy for years, and almost every one of those projects needs an underground fire line before the sprinkler system above grade means anything. It's the piece of the project most likely to get scheduled wrong, because it looks like plumbing work but it isn't governed like plumbing work.

## Why the Underground Fire Line Gets Its Own Rules

The underground fire line runs from the public water main to the riser where your fire sprinkler system actually starts. In Colorado, installing that line requires a specific state registration: **Fire Suppression System Contractor - Underground**, issued by the [Colorado Division of Fire Prevention and Control](https://dfpc.colorado.gov/fire-suppression-system-contractors) under 8 CCR 1507-11. It's a separate credential from the license that lets a contractor install sprinkler piping above ground, and a contractor registered only for underground work is not permitted to touch any other part of the system.

**A general plumbing contractor cannot install your fire line, even if they install your domestic water service on the same project.** The state carves this out specifically because underground fire main work follows [NFPA 24](https://www.nfpa.org/product/nfpa-24-standard-for-the-installation-of-private-fire-service-mains-and-their-appurtenances/p0024code), the standard for private fire service mains, not the plumbing code.

## Aurora's Permit Process

The City of Aurora requires a separate plan review and building permit for fire protection systems, distinct from the general building permit for the structure itself. That applies to the underground fire line, the sprinkler system above it, standpipes, and fire alarm work alike. Aurora's Building Division publishes an average plan review timeline of about 40 working days for a full commercial submittal cycle, roughly 7.7 calendar weeks, accounting for the initial review and one round of corrections, according to the [City of Aurora's commercial plan review checklist](https://cdnsm5-hosted.civiclive.com/UserFiles/Servers/Server_1881137/File/Business%20Services/Development%20Center/Permits/Building%20and%20Life%20Safety%20Permits/Forms%20and%20Applications/002396.pdf). GCs building a schedule around a single 2-3 week review window are setting themselves up for a collision with the sprinkler contractor's timeline.

**Build the fire protection permit into your critical path from day one, not as a follow-on once the building permit clears.**

## What Gets Inspected

Once the line goes in, it doesn't just get buried and forgotten. A hydrostatic test confirms the pipe and joints hold pressure without leaking, and the line gets flushed to clear out construction debris before it's ever connected to a live sprinkler system. A Contractor's Material and Test Certificate documents the results, and copies go to both the property owner and the fire inspector of record. Skip this step, or use a contractor who can't produce this certificate, and the fire marshal will not sign off on the connection to the riser.

Registered underground contractors are required to keep current copies of NFPA 24, along with NFPA 13 and NFPA 22, accessible to their crews, and the registration exists specifically so unqualified installers can't connect a fire suppression system to a public water main.

## Coordinating With the Rest of the Project

The underground fire line usually goes in early, before the slab, which means it has to be coordinated with excavation, other utility trenching, and the building's foundation schedule. On a lot of Aurora projects, this is also the point where the fire protection contractor and the site utility contractor need to be talking directly, not just relaying information through the GC. A misaligned tie-in point or a riser location that doesn't match the approved fire protection plans is an expensive problem to catch after the concrete is poured.

If your project includes a backflow assembly on the fire line, that gets tested and documented separately from the underground line itself, and it needs its own [certified backflow tester](/services/backflow-testing/) before the system is placed in service.

## What This Means for Your Project

If you're a GC bidding or scheduling a commercial project in Aurora, confirm two things before you lock the schedule: that your fire protection subcontractor's underground crew holds the Colorado Fire Suppression System Contractor - Underground registration, and that the fire protection permit is submitted in parallel with the building permit, not after it. Both of those steps, done early, are what keep an underground fire line from becoming the reason your final inspection gets pushed.
