At some point, every commercial fire sprinkler system in Denver needs to come offline. Sometimes it's planned work: a tenant build-out, a pipe repair, a valve replacement, a five-year internal inspection. Sometimes it's unexpected: a burst pipe, a failed component, emergency repairs after a freeze event. Either way, the moment your sprinkler system is out of service, a formal process begins whether you know about it or not.

That process, defined under [NFPA 25](https://www.nfpa.org/codes-and-standards/nfpa-25-standard-for-the-inspection-testing-and-maintenance-of-water-based-fire-protection-systems/about) and enforced by the Denver Fire Department, exists because the period when a building has no fire suppression is a period of elevated risk. How you manage that period determines whether you're in compliance and whether your insurance coverage holds.

## What Counts as an Impairment

An impairment is any condition that renders all or part of your fire sprinkler system unable to function as designed. That includes:

- A closed control valve that takes any part of the system out of service
- A dry pipe valve or pre-action valve that is out of service for maintenance or testing
- A section of pipe that has been removed, cut, or capped during renovation work
- A system that has been partially or fully drained and not yet restored to service
- A fire pump that is out of service when it is the primary water supply for the system

A system does not need to be fully offline to be impaired. If a control valve serves one of four zones in your building and that valve is closed for a week while a contractor does work, that zone is under an impairment condition for that week.

## Planned vs. Emergency Impairments

NFPA 25 distinguishes between the two, and the notification requirements are slightly different.

**Planned impairments** are scheduled in advance. You know the work is coming, you know which parts of the system will be affected, and you know the anticipated duration. For a planned impairment, the required steps are:

- Notify the AHJ. In Denver, that means notifying the Denver Fire Department before the system goes out of service. The required lead time varies by jurisdiction and by the size of the impairment, but notifying with at least 24 hours advance is standard practice for planned work.
- Notify your insurance carrier. Most commercial property policies require notification when the fire suppression system is impaired. Failing to notify can affect your coverage if a fire occurs during the impairment period.
- Determine whether a fire watch is required. For impairments that affect a significant portion of the building, Denver Fire Department may require a staffed fire watch during the outage.
- Assign an impairment coordinator. Under NFPA 25, someone must be specifically designated to oversee the impairment, ensure the system is restored as quickly as possible, and confirm all notifications have been made.

**Emergency impairments** occur without warning, typically from pipe failure, freeze damage, or an unexpected equipment failure. The same notifications are required, but the timeline compresses: you notify the AHJ and insurance carrier as soon as you're aware of the impairment, not before.

## Fire Watch Requirements

A fire watch is exactly what it sounds like: a person physically patrolling the affected area of the building at defined intervals to detect fire conditions while the sprinkler system is unable to respond. It's not a paperwork exercise. It requires a person, walking the space, on a documented schedule, while the system is down.

Denver Fire Department determines fire watch requirements based on the occupancy type, the area affected, and the expected duration of the impairment. For small, short-duration planned impairments in low-occupancy spaces, a fire watch may not be required. For a significant system outage in an occupied building, a staffed fire watch during all occupied hours is a standard requirement.

Fire watches are operationally disruptive. For occupied commercial buildings, they can mean dedicated staff or a contracted security presence during business hours for the duration of the outage. This cost adds up quickly, which is why minimizing impairment duration is not just a compliance matter but a direct operational expense.

## Keeping Impairments Short

The single most effective way to manage impairment costs is to minimize how long the system is actually out of service. For planned work, that means:

- Coordinating all required work so it happens during a single outage window rather than multiple partial outages
- Staging parts, tools, and personnel in advance so the actual out-of-service time is as short as possible
- Scheduling impairments during low-occupancy periods when fire watch requirements are less demanding
- Having the system tested and restored to full service before the contractor leaves the site

Emergency impairments are harder to control, but having a relationship with a licensed fire protection contractor who can respond quickly makes a material difference in how long an unexpected outage lasts.

## Documentation and Records

NFPA 25 requires that every impairment be documented. The documentation includes the date and time the system went out of service, which components or zones were affected, what work was performed, all notifications made, fire watch records if a watch was required, and the date and time the system was returned to full service.

That documentation belongs in your fire protection records alongside your inspection and testing reports. If the Denver Fire Department or your insurance carrier asks about an impairment event, you need to be able to show the full record, not just the repair invoice.

## What This Means for Your Building

Property managers who handle impairments informally, letting contractors shut down zones without notification or documentation, are exposed to both regulatory and insurance risk. The Denver Fire Department takes impairment management seriously, and insurance carriers do as well.
